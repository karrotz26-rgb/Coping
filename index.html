Grounding Guide & Physiology

Explore the mental and physical pillars of grounding. Toggle between the subsections below to explore questions, somatic senses, or co-regulation breathing.

The 5 Core Anchors

This section breaks down the five critical orientation questions from the source report. Click on each question to understand its specific psychological purpose and how to apply the sensory integration tips (See, Hear, Touch) to maximize effectiveness.

Acknowledge your fundamental identity. State your full name clearly. This establishes your primary sense of self before evaluating the environment.

💡

Quick Tip Integration: As you say your name, touch something physically solid (like a desk or your own hands) to connect your identity to a physical sensation.

Orient yourself in time. State the current day, date, month, and year. Trauma or anxiety often pulls the mind into the past or future; this forces temporal presentness.

💡

Quick Tip Integration: Look for a clock, watch, or calendar. See the numbers to visually validate the time you are stating out loud.

Define your physical location. Start broad (city, state) and narrow down to your exact room or spot. This builds a spatial container for your awareness.

💡

Quick Tip Integration: See the surroundings around you. Notice the specifics or nature nearby to firmly plant your mind in this specific location.

Describe your what you are doing right now. "I am sitting on a chair," "I am holding a phone." This bridges the gap between your physical body and your conscious thought.

💡

Quick Tip Integration: Hear the sounds generated by your actions—the rustle of clothing, the hum of an appliance, or your own breathing.

The crucial concluding assessment. Objectively evaluate immediate physical danger. Answering "Yes" signals the amygdala to down-regulate the fight-or-flight response.

💡

Quick Tip Integration: Say out loud "I am safe." If you don't feel calmer yet, the core instruction is to repeat the entire list from question 1.

The 5 Somatic Senses (5-4-3-2-1)

This section breaks down the five sensory systems used during somatic orientation. Click on each sense below to explore its specific physiological grounding effect and discover how physical focus interrupts looping patterns of emotional distress.

Consciously shift your vision outward to scan your environment. Registering small details—like patterns on a surface, reflections of light, or the shape of furniture—forces the brain to allocate cognitive processing resources away from internal worry loops and back into objective reality.

💡

Quick Tip Integration: Pick an arbitrary color (such as green or orange) and challenge yourself to quickly locate five objects of that exact hue in your room.

Focus on physical touchpoints and external physical pressures. Notice the somatic weight of your body settling into your seat, your clothing on your skin, or the temperature of the air. Activating tactile sensory receptors stimulates the somatosensory cortex and grounds your energy bodily.

💡

Quick Tip Integration: Gently run your fingers along the nearest texture (like wood grain, fabric, or a cool metal object). Direct your full attention to describing that exact feeling in your mind.

Filter out the volume of internal thoughts by listening intently for external sound waves. Tune into background sounds you typically overlook: the low hum of electronics, distant cars outside, the wind, or the rhythm of your breath. Listening trains your focus outward.

💡

Quick Tip Integration: Try to alternate your attention between the most distant sound you can possibly find, and then the closest sound (such as your own breathing).

Take a slow, deep breath to locate any scents in your immediate environment. Because olfactory signals bypass major processing centers and hook straight into your limbic brain (emotional and memory centers), smells can evoke incredibly swift resets to our emotional state.

💡

Quick Tip Integration: Keep a tube of scented lotion, a candle, or mild essential oils nearby to act as dedicated olfactory anchors during periods of high tension.

Examine any lingering tastes inside your mouth or take note of the neutral, moist environment of your tongue. Taste, though subtle, serves as an ultimate grounding point because it requires internal physical awareness to process.

💡

Quick Tip Integration: Sip a splash of cool water or chew a strong peppermint to produce a vivid gustatory shock that quickly refocuses your attention.

The Benefits of Deep Breathing

Slow, paced breathing is a direct physiological trigger for safety. Understanding how your body responds to deliberate breath control helps you leverage these co-regulation tools more effectively.

Deep breathing stimulates the vagus nerve, which acts as the brake pedal for your nervous system. By signaling the parasympathetic nervous system, it slows down your heart rate and prompts a state of resting calm.

💡

Somatic Insight: Making your exhalations longer than your inhalations (as practiced in our Bubble Breathing tool) maximizes vagal tone and speeds up neural relaxation.

When you are highly anxious, your body floods with adrenaline and cortisol. Deep diaphragmatic breathing sends an immediate biochemical signal to the brain that you are safe, turning off the alarm systems and allowing cortisol levels to normalize.

💡

Physiological Tip: Breathing deep into the belly (rather than shallow chest breathing) ensures maximum lung expansion and triggers quicker biochemical recovery.

Panic and extreme stress cause mild hyperventilation, narrowing blood vessels and reducing oxygen flow to the prefrontal cortex—the logical, reasoning part of your brain. Deep, controlled breathing restores optimal blood flow, helping you think clearly and maintain control over overwhelming thoughts.

💡

Cognitive Insight: Grounding exercises require a clear mind to complete. Restoring cognitive function through breathing makes sensory and identity exercises much easier to perform.

By regulating your breathing rate, you improve your Heart Rate Variability (HRV)—the metric of how resilient your heart is to stress. Better HRV correlates directly with emotional stability, psychological resilience, and somatic grounding.

💡

Long-term Resilience: Practicing paced breathing even for a few minutes daily helps train your nervous system to recover much faster from surprise panic attacks.

CBT Guide & the Cognitive Model

Learn how your thoughts and beliefs shape how you feel and what you do. Tap a topic below to start: the cognitive model, automatic thoughts, rules and assumptions, or core beliefs.

The Cognitive Model

Your beliefs sit underneath the thoughts that pop up in the moment. Those thoughts then shape how you feel, what you do, and how your body reacts. Tap each level to see what it means, with an example.

We react to how we see a situation, not to the situation itself.

Core beliefs

Your deepest view of yourself, other people, or the world

↓

Intermediate beliefs

The rules, attitudes, and assumptions you live by

↓

Situation

What happens

↓

Automatic thoughts

Quick words or pictures that pop into your mind

↓

Reactions

Feelings · Actions · Body sensations

Source: Judith Beck, Cognitive Behavior Therapy: Basics and Beyond, 2nd ed., chapters 3 and 9–14.

The deepest level. It is a big-picture belief about yourself, other people, or the world. It feels absolute, is hard to shake, and goes further than the facts. It usually starts early in life.

💬

Example: “I’m incompetent.”

Rules, attitudes, and assumptions that grow out of a core belief. You may never have said them out loud, but they guide how you cope.

💬

Examples: Attitude: “It’s terrible to fail.” Rule: “Give up if it’s too hard.” Assumption: “If I try something difficult, I’ll fail.”

The top level. These are quick words or pictures that pop into your mind in a certain moment. They feel true, so we tend to believe them without checking.

💬

Example: “I’ll never finish this paper.”

Therapy usually starts with automatic thoughts. They are the easiest to notice and test. Later, as you build skills and trust, you can work on rules and assumptions, and then on core beliefs.

⚠️

Why go in this order: Core beliefs are deeply held. Questioning them too soon can feel unconvincing and get in the way of working together.

Working with Automatic Thoughts

Automatic thoughts are the easiest to notice, so they are a good place to start. Tap each step to learn how to spot them, check them, and respond to them.

The key question is “What was going through my mind just then?” Ask it when you notice your mood change, or when you think back to an upsetting moment.

If nothing comes to mind, try picturing the moment again in detail, or act it out with your therapist. You can also ask what the situation meant to you, or try a guess and see if it fits.

🔥

Find the “hot” thought: Look for the thought linked to your strongest feeling. Rate how much you believe it (0–100%) and how strong the feeling is.

Don’t try to argue yourself out of it. Get curious and look at the thought with these questions:

  1. What is the evidence for this thought? Against it?
  2. Is there another explanation or way to see it?
  3. What’s the worst that could happen, and how would I cope? The best? The most realistic?
  4. What happens if I keep believing this thought? What could happen if I changed it?
  5. What would I tell a friend or family member in this situation?
  6. What should I do?
  • Distorted: the thought doesn’t match the facts.
  • True, but the conclusion goes too far: the facts are right, but what you take from them isn’t.
  • True, but unhelpful: the thought is right, but dwelling on it makes things harder.
💡

When a thought is true: Focus on solving the problem, or ask what the thought means to you.

When you’re done, rate again how much you believe the thought and how strong the feeling is. Then write down a more balanced response. You can use your therapy notes, a coping card, a Thought Record, or the simpler Testing Your Thoughts worksheet. Writing it down helps you use the skill during the week.

Working with Rules & Assumptions

Rules, attitudes, and assumptions guide how you cope. Tap each step to learn how to find them, pick which ones to work on, and change them.

  • Notice when a thought sounds like a belief or a rule.
  • Finish the sentence: “If I don’t do well, then…”
  • Ask yourself: “What rule am I living by here?”
  • Look for themes that keep coming up in your thoughts.
⬇️

Downward arrow: Take a key thought and ask, “If that were true, what would it mean? What would it say about me?” Keep asking until you reach the belief underneath.

Focus on beliefs that matter most and that you believe strongly.

💡

Skip for now: Beliefs you only slightly believe (around 20%).

Turn rules and attitudes into “if…then” statements. These are easier to test. List the pros and cons of keeping the belief. Then build a more helpful belief, and each day rate how much you believe the old one and the new one.

Ways to do this:

  • Asking questions about the belief, like the ones for automatic thoughts
  • Testing the belief in real life (behavioral experiments)
  • Placing yourself on a scale instead of thinking all-or-nothing
  • Role plays where your “head” side talks back to your “gut” side
  • Asking whether you would judge someone else the same way
  • Acting “as if” you already held the new belief
  • Hearing how your therapist has handled similar beliefs

Working with Core Beliefs

Core beliefs are the deepest level. They are usually worked on later, once you have built skills and trust. Tap each topic to learn about the types, the steps, and the tools.

Most core beliefs fit into one of three types. Notice which one shows up for you:

  • Helpless: “I’m incompetent / weak / a failure.” These are about getting things done, keeping yourself safe, or achieving.
  • Unlovable: “I’m unlikable / defective / bound to be rejected.”
  • Worthless: “I’m bad / worthless / dangerous to others.” These are about whether you are a good person.
  1. Your therapist forms an early guess about which type fits.
  2. Together you narrow down the exact belief.
  3. Your therapist shares it as a guess, and you say if it fits or correct it.
  4. You learn how core beliefs work and notice how yours shows up now.
  5. You build and strengthen a new, balanced belief.
  6. You weaken the old one by looking at where it started in childhood, what has kept it going, and how it causes problems today.

A core belief is an idea, not a fact. It can feel true and still be wrong. And it can be tested.

🔍

The filter: You likely learned the belief early in life. A filter keeps it alive: you notice things that fit it, and you ignore or downplay things that don’t.

The same tools used for rules and assumptions, plus:

  • The Core Belief Worksheet, an ongoing log of evidence for the new belief and new ways to see the old one
  • Comparing yourself with someone at the far end of the belief (extreme contrasts)
  • Stories and metaphors
  • Looking back at the evidence from each period of your life
  • Revisiting painful early memories with imagery or role play to see them in a new way
  • Coping cards
❤️

Head and heart: Many people say, “I know it in my head, but I don’t feel it.” That’s why this work uses both thinking exercises and hands-on experiences.
